Pause here: commas after introductory phrases

You have probably written “To sum up we can say…” and felt something was missing. Learn the rule, test it in six different tasks and see your final score at the end.

Theory

The main rule

When a sentence starts with an introductory element, a word or group of words that comes before the main clause, we put a comma after it. The comma marks the pause between the introduction and the real message (subject + verb).

To sum up, teenagers need more sleep than adults.
In conclusion, recycling saves energy.
However, not everyone agrees.

Six kinds of introductions

Concluding phrases

To sum up, In conclusion, To conclude, All in all.

To conclude, we need stronger rules.

Transition words

However, Therefore, Meanwhile, Finally, Unfortunately.

Therefore, we left early.

Prepositional phrases

After the match, Despite the rain, In my opinion.

After the match, the fans went home.

Subordinate clauses

Because it was late, Although she was tired, If you try,

If you practise, you will improve.

-ing / -ed phrases

Opening the door, Tired of waiting,

Opening the door, Sam smiled.

Interjections and fillers

Well, Of course, Honestly, Yes,

Of course, I will help.

When there is no comma

If the main clause comes first and the extra information comes after it, we usually do not need a comma.

✓ Because it rained, we stayed in. (introduction first, so a comma)
✓ We stayed in because it rained. (main clause first, so no comma)

Also: a very short phrase (On Monday we have a test.) may skip the comma, but a comma is never wrong and is the safer choice in school writing. In this lesson, the tasks use clear cases.

Common mistakes

✗ To sum up the story is about courage. (missing comma)

✗ To sum, up the story is about courage. (comma in the wrong place)

✗ However, the plan works, (comma at the end, not needed)

✓ To sum up, the story is about courage.

Quick test: find the subject and the verb of the main clause. Everything before them is the introduction, so put the comma just before the subject.
